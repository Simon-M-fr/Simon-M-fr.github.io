Is the Bar Higher for Female Scholars? Evidence from Career Steps in Economics (with Niels Johannesen)
Published in Journal of Public Economics (2026)

Abstract: Do gender disparities in academia reflect that female scholars are held to higher standards than males? We address this question by comparing the academic achievements of male and female scholars in economics who make the same career step. Across four domains—faculty appointments, network invitations, grant awards and editor appointments—we find no evidence that standards are higher for females. By contrast, the average female has fewer citations and publications than the average male who makes the same career step. In most domains, this reflects a gender gap for “marginal” scholars, consistent with a lower bar for females.

What Drives Corporate Philanthropy? Novel Evidence from Tax Data
(with Julia Cagé, Agathe Denis, Malka Guillot and Camille Urvoy)

Abstract: How responsive is corporate philanthropy to tax incentives? We combine exhaustive French corporate tax records for 2003–2022 with itemized donor–recipient data for firms donating more than €10,000 annually in 2019–2022. Two reforms raised eligible-donation caps for firms below sharp sales thresholds. Difference-in-differences estimates show that the reforms increased corporate donations. Diff-in-bunching estimates imply tax-price elasticities of −0.391 and −0.461. Responses are substantially larger among small and medium-sized enterprises. Matched donor–recipient data provide suggestive evidence of greater responsiveness when giving is concentrated on one recipient and for foundations rather than associations.

NGO Pressure and Corporate Philanthropy
(with Gabrielle Fack)

Abstract: Corporate philanthropy is often framed as a pillar of corporate social responsibility, yet whether it responds to external pressure on firm conduct, and whether such a response is genuine or symbolic, remains untested. We study this question both theoretically, through a simple conceptual framework of reputational repair via corporate giving, and empirically. Combining French administrative tax-credit data on corporate giving with the Sigwatch database of NGO campaigns, as well as data on Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) scores from LSEG, we exploit the timing of campaign onset (2011–2019) in a staggered difference-in-differences design to provide the first causal evidence that NGO campaigns, which increase external scrutiny on specific firms, raise corporate donations. We first establish that NGO campaigns are rarely isolated events, as most firms are targeted repeatedly. Campaign pressure accumulates steadily in the years following a firm’s first campaign, reflected in a consistent mirror pattern with firms’ ESG Controversies Score, a medium-run measure of media scrutiny, which deteriorates persistently as pressure accumulates. Our results show that, over the same horizon, targeted firms gradually raise annual donations by around 60% of pre-campaign levels, entirely on the intensive margin. Firms’ disclosure-based ESG Score, however, does not move over the same horizon, a pattern consistent with greenwashing, in which giving would not change firms’ actual practices.

Private Equity Buyouts and Corporate Philanthropy
(Single-authored)

Abstract: What determines corporate philanthropy? Two views compete. Under the strategic CSR view, donations are value-enhancing investments that build stakeholder trust, firm reputation, and social capital. Under the discretionary spending view, they reflect the idiosyncratic preferences of owners and managers, and are cut when governance tightens. We exploit private-equity leveraged buyouts as governance shocks to answer this question. Using French administrative data linked to 1,309 buyouts between 2005 and 2021, we document a large and persistent decline in corporate giving following acquisition, in both donation amounts and the probability of donating. We find no robust evidence that the decline is offset by higher giving at acquiring or parent firms, suggesting that it reflects a net reduction rather than systematic reallocation within the corporate group. The decline occurs despite increases in employment, profitability and size at target firms. Among firms that donated prior to acquisition, the decline is significantly larger for large donors than for small donors. We find no statistically precise heterogeneity across groups defined by post-buyout leverage increases, industry-level consumer exposure, local labor-market conditions, or buyer nationality. The heterogeneity patterns are consistent with a role for owner-specific preferences, but they do not identify a unique mechanism.
